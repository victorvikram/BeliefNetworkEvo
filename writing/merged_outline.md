# Outline

1. **The Problem.** Correlation networks cannot tell us *how individuals think*. They are missing two key ingredients:
- causal structure: all we have is information about which beliefs tend to occur together: we don't know what caused them to do so 
- isolation from environment: even if we could infer some causal structure from the correlations, we wouldn't know if the causes were "environmental" or "in-brain"  

2. **The Solution.** Yet, correlation networks do tell us about the patterns of beliefs in the population. These matter for three reasons:
- correlations give predictive power
- correlations allow us to infer something about the underlying causal structure
- correlations have causal power in the world
Indeed, these benefits hold even for very diverse ontologies of beliefs: belief box? High dimensional embedding space? No problem.

3. **Why correlations matter.**
- they give us predictive power: 
    - (EXAMPLE) constructing political parties by satisfying correlations, show how modules would affect this. show how connectivity and modular structure map onto a dimensionally reduced picture of beliefs
- they allow us to infer something about the underlying causal structure
    - describe what they allow us to infer: no correlation => no causation, probably, different networks => different underlying causal structures
    - say why this is important -- potential polarization, even if people start by having the same beliefs 
    - (EXAMPLE) inferring potential polarization (if possible)
- they exert their own causal power:
    - wedging and polarizing coalitions
    - shaping problem solving
    - shaping responses to discourse
    - in-brainification 
    - (EXAMPLE) would be good to give an example that covers two-ish of these things. either some kind of potential wedge, or that demonstrates evidence of how co-occurence shapes problem solving.
- *Synthesis* Different correlation networks means those subpopulations will react differently to different stimuli. That can come from either causal connections between beliefs (potential polarization), or correlations that have a causal impact on the world (wedging, problem-solving). 

4. **Why the specific nature of beliefs doesn't matter (that much)** Some people may worry that belief networks seem to imply a particular ontology of beliefs; that they are discrete objects stored in the mind like a set of mutually-referencing files. 
- But belief networks don't assume this; they just assume persistence and objectivity.
- However, if we ditch the discrete-entity notion of belief, than we have to concede that the belief network is not an objective structure, it exists only relative to the questions that were asked -- it depends on how the researcher chooses to discretize the space. Therefore, absolute metrics mean little.
